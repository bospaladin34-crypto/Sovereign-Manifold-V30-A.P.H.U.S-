# OliO: ODE-based Linear Transition Operator for Self-Supervised Time Series Forecasting

# OliO: ODE-based Linear Transition Operator for Self-Supervised Time Series Forecasting

Self-supervised representation learning has advanced time series forecasting by capturing robust features from unlabeled data. However, existing contrastive and masking-based methods often struggle to explicitly model the underlying temporal flow, either by treating time lag as noise or by disrupting the inherent temporal dynamics essential for forecasting. To address these limitations, we propose OliO (ODE-based Linear Transition Operator), a plug-and-play self-supervised learning method that learns bidirectional temporal flows as a continuous and structurally consistent dynamical system. OliO introduces a transition operator derived from linear Ordinary Differential Equations (ODEs) to align latent representations across arbitrary time shifts. By imposing a strictly upper triangular constraint on the transition matrix, we mathematically ensure numerical stability and enforce a robust polynomial boundary that effectively captures long-term dependencies while preventing the exponential divergence typically found in unconstrained ODE-based models. This structural constraint induces a temporally coherent representation space that preserves the underlying flow essential for accurate forecasting. Extensive experiments across various backbone architectures and benchmarks demonstrate that OliO achieves significant performance gains, providing Mean Squared Error (MSE) reductions of up to 5.75% over the most competitive grid-searched SOTA baselines. The code is available at https://github.com/eai-lab/OliO.

## 1 Introduction

Time series forecasting plays a crucial role in various real-world applications, such as energy consumption planning, traffic analysis, healthcare monitoring, and financial market prediction [31, 34]. However, building accurate forecasting models remains a significant challenge due to the complex temporal variations and high-dimensional nature of massive time series data. Recently, to address these challenges, self-supervised learning (SSL), which learns meaningful representations from unlabeled data, has been actively adopted in the time series domain. Representative approaches include contrastive learning [32, 7, 30], which learns representations by regulating the similarity of related temporal views, and masked modeling [13, 6, 5], which learns through reconstruction of masked observations.

A central question for forecasting is how the temporal displacement between historical windows should be represented during self-supervised pre-training. Two overlapping windows may share many observations while representing different temporal states. Their temporal displacement specifies both how far apart the windows are and which window precedes the other. Contrastive and masked-modeling objectives provide supervision through representation similarity or observation reconstruction. SoftCLT [12] additionally uses temporal distance to weight similarity, but does not explicitly learn a transition conditioned on the signed lag between the two windows. This motivates an objective that explicitly learns where a historical representation should move under the pair’s actual signed temporal displacement.

## 2 Related work

### 2.1 Self-supervised learning for time series

Contrastive learning and masked modeling provide effective supervision for learning time-series representations without forecasting labels. Contrastive methods such as TS2Vec [32], TS-TCC [7], and CoST [30] regulate similarity between related temporal views while separating negative pairs. Masked-modeling methods such as Ti-MAE [13] and SimMTM [6] learn representations through observation reconstruction. These objectives capture useful temporal structure, but do not explicitly parameterize how one historical representation should transition toward another under their actual signed temporal displacement.

Non-contrastive methods such as SimSiam [3] and BYOL [8] learn by predicting a target representation from another view, with gradients stopped through the target branch. OliO builds on this alignment mechanism. The distinction lies in the input and role of the prediction module: conventional view-alignment heads receive neither the actual temporal separation of two historical windows nor its direction. In contrast, OliO takes the pair-specific signed lag as an input and explicitly incorporates it into the transition module’s formulation to transition the source representation before alignment. Thus, its temporal supervision comes from the actual displacement between the windows, in addition to prediction across the two branches.

Time-aware methods incorporate temporal relations more directly. SoftCLT [12] uses temporal distance to weight a contrastive similarity objective. The distance controls the strength of attraction between representations, rather than a transformation toward a lag-conditioned destination; exchanging temporal order leaves the unsigned distance unchanged. TimeSiam [5] incorporates temporally separated subseries and past-to-current reconstruction. It therefore exploits temporal context, while supervising observation reconstruction rather than a pair-specific signed transition between independently encoded historical representations. OliO instead conditions a learnable bidirectional transition on the actual signed lag and aligns each transitioned representation with the independently encoded representation of the other window. This makes temporal displacement determine a representation-level transition, rather than only the strength of similarity or the context for reconstructing observations.

### 2.2 Relation to latent dynamics and operator learning

Latent dynamics and operator-learning methods learn representations for trajectory generation [24], forecasting [28], and evolution-operator estimation and analysis [26]. OliO draws on latent-transition modeling to define a forecasting-oriented pre-training objective: the actual signed lag between independently encoded historical windows supervises bidirectional representation transitions. Its transition module is used to train forecasting representations and is then discarded together with the projection head; downstream prediction is performed by the original backbone. OliO therefore uses established latent-dynamics tools to construct signed temporal supervision for forecasting representations.

### 4.4 Ablation studies

Actual signed temporal displacement. Table 5 tests which aspects of the signed lag provide useful supervision on PatchTST and iTransformer across 48 settings. Each control changes only the supplied lag relative to the original formulation. Fixed retains the correct direction but replaces the pair-specific magnitude with a constant. Its 1.10% higher MSE indicates that the actual separation carries useful information beyond temporal order. Random Magnitude retains the correct sign but supplies a mismatched magnitude. The 2.57% increase shows that varying the conditioning value alone does not recover the benefit of matching it to the actual window separation. Random Direction preserves the actual magnitude and opposite signs between branches, but randomizes their correspondence to earlier–later order. Its 1.82% increase isolates the value of assigning signs according to the true temporal direction, beyond simply using opposite signs in the two branches. Unsigned supplies the correct magnitude with a positive sign in both branches. Its 4.86% increase, the largest among these controls, supports distinguishing forward from backward transport. Together, the controls support conditioning the transition on both the actual separation and its temporal direction. With the transition module and alignment objective held fixed, these results indicate that the actual temporal relation between windows provides useful supervision for forecasting.

Exponential transport and additive bias. Table 6 examines the contributions of matrix transport and the additive offset over the same 48 settings. Bias-only removes matrix transport, applying the same displacement to every representation at a given signed lag. Its 3.83% higher MSE supports allowing the displacement to depend on the source representation. K-only retains exponential transport but removes the offset. The 1.40% increase indicates that a learned translation complements the multiplicative transformation of the source state; neither component alone matches the complete transition. MLP tests whether a generic nonlinear mapping can replace the explicit transition structure. Its 3.13% increase supports the proposed structured parameterization over this nonlinear alternative. Spec-K replaces the strictly upper-triangular generator with a full generator using spectral normalization. It remains numerically stable but has 1.18% higher MSE. Thus, the performance gap persists when both variants train without numerical divergence, supporting a contribution of the generator structure beyond avoiding numerical failure. ## 5 Future work and discussion

The signed-lag controls (Table 5) favor conditioning transitions on the actual temporal separation and direction. The overlap sweeps and matched masking control (Table 9) suggest that shared observations alone do not explain the forecasting gains. Together, these findings support learning temporal change by aligning window representations through transitions matched to their observed temporal relation.

The ODE determines OliO’s homogeneous matrix transition, while the shared signed offset is parameterized separately. The complete affine map need not be the flow of an autonomous affine ODE. Each lag-specific affine map is invertible, but the opposite-lag transition need not be its inverse. Bidirectional alignment therefore supervises both temporal directions without imposing exact inverse composition. The projection and transition modules provide pre-training supervision and are discarded for downstream forecasting.

Koopman-based methods [11, 16] motivate representing nonlinear observations in coordinates suited to linear transport. OliO uses a nonlinear projection head for this purpose, but a finite-dimensional learned projection does not guarantee exact linearization of arbitrary dynamics. Its nilpotent generator models finite-lag polynomial transients rather than sustained periodic orbits. A single state-independent generator can also be restrictive when transitions vary across states or regimes; state-dependent transport is a possible extension.

Beyond forecasting, signed-lag supervision may be useful for other time-series tasks. SoftCLT [12] has demonstrated the value of distance-aware representations for classification, motivating further evaluation of OliO for structural-break detection and activity classification on benchmarks such as UCR [4] and UEA [1]. Such transfer remains an empirical question rather than a consequence of the finite-polynomial transition theorem.

## 6 Conclusion

We introduced OliO, a self-supervised pre-training framework that learns bidirectional transitions between representations of historical windows using their actual signed temporal displacement. Its transition combines an ODE-derived matrix exponential with a shared offset scaled by the signed lag. A strictly upper-triangular generator makes the transition exactly expressible as a finite polynomial in lag in both temporal directions. Across three forecasting backbones and six datasets, OliO improves on TimesURL, the strongest SSL baseline by aggregate MSE, in 58 of 72 settings, with up to 5.75% lower MSE. Frozen-backbone probing and zero-shot evaluation provide additional evidence of representation quality and transferability. Controlled ablations support using the actual lag magnitude and direction, combining exponential transport with an additive offset, and supervising both temporal directions. The projection and transition modules are discarded after pre-training, so downstream forecasting retains the original backbone’s architecture and inference cost. These findings support using observed temporal displacement to guide representation learning for forecasting.
